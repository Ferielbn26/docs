Every living organism needs energy to survive, and that energy is constantly moving — from the sun to a blade of grass, from grass to a grasshopper, from grasshopper to frog, from frog to hawk. These feeding relationships form the structural backbone of every ecosystem on Earth. EcoLearn's interactive Food Web Simulator makes these invisible connections visible, letting you build, modify, and stress-test food webs to discover just how tightly species depend on one another. Before you dive into the tool, this guide will give you the conceptual grounding you need to make sense of what you see.

## Food Chains vs. Food Webs

A **food chain** is the simplest way to represent feeding relationships — a single, linear sequence from producer to apex predator:

```
Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk
```

Food chains are useful for illustrating the flow of energy at a glance, but they're an oversimplification. Real ecosystems are far messier: most animals eat multiple species, many predators are also prey, and energy pathways branch and loop in complex ways. That's where **food webs** come in.

A food web is a network of overlapping and interconnected food chains within an ecosystem. Rather than a single line, it looks more like a tangled lattice — and that complexity is precisely what gives ecosystems their stability. If one food chain breaks (a species disappears), the web has other pathways that can partially compensate.

| Feature | Food Chain | Food Web |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Linear, single pathway | Networked, multiple pathways |
| Realism | Simplified | More accurate |
| Resilience shown | No | Yes — redundant connections buffer against loss |
| Best used for | Introducing energy flow | Modeling real ecosystem dynamics |

## Trophic Levels

Organisms in a food web are organized into **trophic levels** based on how far their energy is removed from the original solar source:

- **Level 1 — Producers:** Plants, algae, and other photosynthetic organisms that convert sunlight into chemical energy. They form the base of every food web and support all life above them.
- **Level 2 — Primary Consumers:** Herbivores that eat producers directly — rabbits, deer, zooplankton, caterpillars.
- **Level 3 — Secondary Consumers:** Animals that prey on primary consumers — foxes, small fish, insectivorous birds.
- **Level 4 and above — Tertiary Consumers and Apex Predators:** Animals at the top of the web with few or no natural predators — wolves, sharks, eagles, orcas.

A critical principle governs how energy moves between these levels: the **10% Rule**. On average, only about 10% of the energy stored at one trophic level is transferred to the next. The remaining 90% is lost as heat through metabolic processes. This is why ecosystems can support vast quantities of plant biomass, moderate numbers of herbivores, fewer carnivores, and only a handful of apex predators — each level up is fundamentally limited by what's available below it.

<Note>
The 10% rule is an approximation — actual transfer efficiency varies from around 5% to 20% depending on the organisms and ecosystem involved. EcoLearn's simulator uses variable efficiency rates so you can see how these differences affect population sizes at each level.
</Note>

## Keystone Species

Some species have an outsized influence on their ecosystem relative to their abundance — remove them, and the entire web can unravel. These are called **keystone species**, named after the central stone in an arch that holds everything else in place.

**Sea otters** are a classic example. Otters prey on sea urchins, keeping their populations in check. Without otters, urchin populations explode and devastate kelp forests by over-grazing. Without kelp, dozens of species that depend on the forest for shelter and food disappear — and the reef becomes an ecological dead zone. Reintroduce the otters, and the kelp forest recovers, pulling dozens of other species back with it.

**Wolves** in Yellowstone tell a similar story. When wolves were reintroduced in 1995 after a 70-year absence, they reduced elk grazing pressure on riverbanks. Vegetation recovered, stabilizing stream banks, lowering water temperatures, and improving conditions for fish, beavers, songbirds, and even the physical shape of rivers — a phenomenon known as a **trophic cascade**.

Understanding keystone species helps you see that protecting biodiversity isn't just about saving individual species in isolation; it's about preserving the relationships between them.

## Using the EcoLearn Food Web Simulator

The Food Web Simulator is one of EcoLearn's most powerful learning tools. Follow these steps to get started and make the most of it:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Navigate to the Simulations section">
    From your EcoLearn dashboard, click **Simulations** in the top navigation bar. This opens the full simulations library, where you can browse all available tools.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Select the Food Web simulation">
    Find the **Food Web** card in the library and click **Launch**. You'll be prompted to choose a starting ecosystem — options include a temperate forest, a coral reef, a freshwater lake, and an open grassland. Select one that interests you, or start with the default temperate forest.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Explore the species network">
    The simulator loads a visual network diagram showing all species in the ecosystem connected by feeding arrows. Hover over any species node to see its trophic level, typical population size, and the species it eats and is eaten by.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add or remove species">
    Use the **Species Panel** on the left to add new species to your web or remove existing ones. Click the **+** button next to any species name to introduce it, or click the **×** button on a node in the diagram to remove it. Watch how the rest of the network responds — population sliders update in real time.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Observe cascade effects">
    After making changes, click **Run Simulation** to watch population dynamics play out over a simulated time period. The graph panel on the right plots population changes over time for every species. Look for trophic cascades — chain reactions triggered by your intervention that ripple up and down the web.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review your results">
    When the simulation completes, click **Summary** to see a breakdown of which species gained or lost population, which relationships were most critical, and what the overall impact on ecosystem stability was. You can export this summary or save it to your EcoLearn portfolio.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
Try **removing a keystone species** — like the apex predator in your chosen ecosystem — and run the simulation. The trophic cascade that follows is one of the most visually striking demonstrations of ecological interdependence you'll find anywhere on EcoLearn. Compare the results to a scenario where you remove a non-keystone species of similar population size and see the difference for yourself.
</Tip>
