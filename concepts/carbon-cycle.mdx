Carbon is the currency of life. Every living cell is built around it, and the way carbon moves between the atmosphere, oceans, soils, and living organisms determines the temperature of our planet and the health of every ecosystem on it. The carbon cycle is both a natural wonder and, increasingly, a system under pressure — human activities have altered its balance in ways that are reshaping Earth's climate. EcoLearn's Carbon Cycle module walks you through this essential process step by step, pairing clear explanations with interactive activities that let you manipulate carbon flows and see the consequences unfold in real time.

## Carbon Reservoirs

Carbon doesn't stay in one place — it moves constantly between large storage pools called **reservoirs**. Understanding where carbon is stored, how much is held in each reservoir, and what processes move it in and out is the starting point for understanding the whole cycle.

| Reservoir | Carbon Stored (approx.) | Key Process |
|---|---|---|
| Atmosphere | ~870 billion tonnes (GtC) | Photosynthesis (removal), respiration and combustion (addition) |
| Oceans | ~38,000 GtC | Ocean absorption of CO₂, marine photosynthesis, dissolution |
| Terrestrial Biosphere | ~2,300 GtC (soils) + ~550 GtC (vegetation) | Photosynthesis, decomposition, soil respiration |
| Fossil Fuels | ~3,700 GtC (recoverable) | Combustion (releases stored carbon back to atmosphere) |

The oceans are by far the largest active reservoir, absorbing enormous quantities of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. Soils store more carbon than all the world's vegetation combined — which is why soil health is a central concern in climate discussions. Fossil fuels represent ancient carbon that was locked away over hundreds of millions of years and is now being released over decades.

## Carbon Fluxes

A **flux** is simply the movement of carbon between reservoirs. Several key fluxes drive the carbon cycle:

**Photosynthesis** is the cycle's great engine of removal. Plants, algae, and cyanobacteria absorb CO₂ from the atmosphere and use solar energy to convert it into organic molecules — sugars, cellulose, and more. Each year, terrestrial and marine photosynthesis together pull roughly 120 billion tonnes of carbon out of the atmosphere.

**Respiration** runs in the opposite direction. All living organisms — including plants — break down organic molecules to release energy, releasing CO₂ back into the atmosphere in the process. Cellular respiration is constant, happening in every living cell every second of every day.

**Decomposition** is nature's recycling system. When organisms die, bacteria and fungi break down their tissues, releasing the carbon stored in them back into the atmosphere and soil. This flux is especially significant in tropical soils and wetlands, where decomposition rates are high.

**Ocean absorption** is a critical buffer. The world's oceans absorb approximately 25–30% of the CO₂ humans emit each year through a combination of physical dissolution at the surface and biological uptake by marine phytoplankton. Without this, atmospheric CO₂ levels — and temperatures — would be rising even faster.

**Fossil fuel combustion** is the cycle's most disruptive modern flux. Burning coal, oil, and natural gas for energy releases carbon that was sequestered from the atmosphere over geological timescales — effectively injecting ancient carbon into the present-day cycle far faster than natural processes can reabsorb it.

## Carbon Cycle and Climate Change

For most of Earth's recent history, the carbon cycle maintained a rough balance: carbon emitted through natural processes was matched by carbon absorbed by plants and oceans. Human activity has fundamentally disrupted that balance.

Since the industrial revolution, humans have released over **1.7 trillion tonnes of CO₂** into the atmosphere — through burning fossil fuels, clearing forests, and changing land use. This additional carbon intensifies the **greenhouse effect**: CO₂ and other greenhouse gases trap heat radiated from Earth's surface, preventing it from escaping to space. The result is a warming planet, with cascading consequences for weather patterns, sea levels, ocean chemistry, and the ecosystems you're studying across EcoLearn.

<Warning>
Atmospheric CO₂ concentration has surpassed **420 parts per million (ppm)** — the highest level in at least 3 million years. Before industrialization, it stood at around 280 ppm. This rapid increase is the primary driver of current climate change, and the carbon cycle is the mechanism through which that change is transmitted to ecosystems worldwide.
</Warning>

Ocean absorption of CO₂ comes with its own cost: as oceans absorb more carbon, they become more acidic — a process called **ocean acidification**. This threatens marine organisms that build shells and skeletons from calcium carbonate, including corals, oysters, and many species of plankton that form the base of marine food webs.

## Exploring the Carbon Cycle in EcoLearn

EcoLearn's **Carbon Cycle module** is part of the main Lessons section and is designed to be completed in two to three study sessions. Here's what you'll find inside:

The module opens with an **animated overview** of the carbon cycle, tracing a single carbon atom from a plant cell through the atmosphere, into an ocean, and eventually into a fossilized deposit — and back out through combustion. This visualization alone makes the abstract concept of carbon fluxes concrete and memorable.

From there, you move into **guided interactive activities**. In the Carbon Flux Builder, you adjust the rates of individual processes — increasing deforestation, boosting ocean absorption, or dialing up fossil fuel emissions — and observe how the atmospheric carbon reservoir responds over decades and centuries. It's a hands-on way to understand why certain policy choices matter more than others.

The module closes with a **Carbon Budget exercise**, where you're given a target atmospheric CO₂ level and asked to achieve it by making decisions about energy sources, land use, and carbon capture. This scenario-based activity brings together everything you've learned and challenges you to think like a climate scientist.

To access the module, click **Lessons** in your dashboard navigation, then select **Carbon Cycle** from the Earth Systems section. No prior knowledge is required — the module builds from first principles, though completing the Ecosystems and Food Webs concepts first will enrich your understanding significantly.
