Ecosystems are the living heart of our planet — complex, interconnected systems where organisms and their environments shape each other in ways that sustain all life on Earth. Whether you're just starting your ecology journey or deepening your knowledge, EcoLearn gives you the tools to explore these systems up close. Through interactive simulations, guided lesson modules, and real-world case studies, you'll move beyond textbook definitions and start seeing ecosystems as the dynamic, ever-changing networks they truly are.

## What Is an Ecosystem?

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms — plants, animals, fungi, and microbes — interacting with each other and with their non-living surroundings as a unified system. Every ecosystem has two essential components: **biotic** (living) elements such as producers, consumers, and decomposers, and **abiotic** (non-living) factors such as sunlight, water, temperature, and soil chemistry. Together, these components cycle nutrients, transfer energy, and maintain conditions that support life.

Ecosystems come in an enormous variety of forms, from the dense canopy of a tropical rainforest to the frozen plains of the Arctic tundra. Here are three iconic examples:

| Ecosystem Type | Biotic Components | Abiotic Factors |
|---|---|---|
| Tropical Rainforest | Canopy trees, jaguars, poison dart frogs, epiphytes, decomposer fungi | High rainfall, warm temperatures, humid air, nutrient-poor soils |
| Coral Reef | Corals, reef fish, sea turtles, algae, invertebrates | Saltwater, water temperature, sunlight penetration, ocean currents |
| Arctic Tundra | Lichens, mosses, caribou, Arctic foxes, migratory birds | Permafrost, extreme cold, low precipitation, short growing season |

Each ecosystem has its own balance — alter one component and you trigger ripple effects throughout the entire system. This sensitivity is exactly what makes ecology so fascinating to study.

## Energy Flow

Energy enters most ecosystems through **producers** — organisms like plants, algae, and cyanobacteria that capture sunlight via photosynthesis and convert it into chemical energy. That energy is then passed along a chain of **consumers**:

- **Primary consumers** (herbivores) eat producers directly — think deer grazing on grass or caterpillars munching leaves.
- **Secondary consumers** (carnivores or omnivores) feed on primary consumers — a fox hunting mice, for example.
- **Tertiary consumers** sit at higher levels of the chain, feeding on secondary consumers — such as a hawk preying on that same fox.

At every stage, **decomposers** — bacteria and fungi — break down dead organic matter and return nutrients to the soil, completing the cycle.

One important rule to keep in mind: only about **10% of energy** is transferred from one trophic level to the next. The rest is lost as heat. This is why ecosystems support far fewer apex predators than herbivores.

## How EcoLearn Covers Ecosystems

EcoLearn structures ecosystem content across three dedicated lesson modules, each designed to build your understanding progressively:

- **Terrestrial Ecosystems** — Covers forests, grasslands, deserts, and tundra, with a focus on soil ecology and land-based food webs.
- **Aquatic Ecosystems** — Explores freshwater and marine environments, including nutrient cycles driven by water movement.
- **Urban Ecosystems** — An often-overlooked module that examines how cities create unique ecological niches and the surprising biodiversity found within them.

Each module pairs readings with **interactive simulations** — including the Population Dynamics and Nutrient Cycle tools — and closes with a **quiz** to consolidate what you've learned. Your progress is tracked on your dashboard, so you can always pick up where you left off.
