Habitat loss and degradation are the leading drivers of biodiversity decline worldwide, yet their consequences can be difficult to visualize from statistics alone. The Habitat Change simulation closes that gap. By letting you control the pace and nature of land-use change — and then watching a biodiversity index decline and a habitat map fragment in real time — it transforms abstract data into something visceral and memorable. You'll come away with a deeper understanding of why conservationists fight to protect even small patches of habitat, and how restoration efforts can begin to reverse damage over time.

## Scenario Types

The simulation offers four distinct scenarios, each representing a real and widespread form of habitat change:

- **Deforestation** — trees are cleared for agriculture, logging, or infrastructure. Watch how forest cover shrinks, edge habitat expands, and forest-dependent species lose viable territory as fragmentation accelerates.
- **Urban Expansion** — low-density development spreads outward from a city center, converting farmland and natural habitat into impervious surfaces. This scenario highlights the tension between human growth and green space preservation.
- **Wetland Drainage** — marshes and floodplains are drained for agriculture or development. This scenario reveals how quickly wetland-dependent species collapse when hydrological connectivity is severed, even if adjacent land remains intact.
- **Climate Warming** — rising temperatures shift habitat suitability across the landscape, forcing species to move, adapt, or disappear. This scenario is unique in that land cover doesn't visibly change — but the biodiversity index responds to thermal stress and phenological mismatches over time.

## Key Variables You Control

Each scenario shares a common set of variables that you configure before running. The table below describes what each one controls.

| Variable               | Description                                                                                     |
|------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Land Conversion Rate   | The percentage of natural habitat converted per decade — higher values accelerate habitat loss  |
| Protected Area Size    | The percentage of the landscape placed under formal protection, where conversion cannot occur   |
| Restoration Effort     | The level of funding and effort directed toward restoring degraded habitat during the simulation |
| Temperature Change     | The total degrees Celsius of warming applied over the full simulation period (relevant for Climate Warming scenario) |
| Species Sensitivity    | How sensitive the simulated species community is to habitat change — high sensitivity amplifies the biodiversity response |

Not all variables are active in every scenario. For example, Temperature Change is only relevant in the Climate Warming scenario, and Land Conversion Rate applies most directly to Deforestation and Urban Expansion. Inactive variables are grayed out automatically when you select your scenario.

## Running a Scenario

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open the Habitat Change Simulation">
    From the EcoLearn navigation bar, go to **Simulations → Habitat Change**. The simulation will load with a default scenario selected.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Choose a Scenario">
    Click one of the four scenario cards — **Deforestation**, **Urban Expansion**, **Wetland Drainage**, or **Climate Warming** — to load its specific landscape, species set, and context description. Read the context panel on the right before proceeding.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Set Your Variables">
    Use the sliders to configure Land Conversion Rate, Protected Area Size, Restoration Effort, Temperature Change (if applicable), and Species Sensitivity. Start with a moderate setting on each variable for your first run to establish a baseline.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Click Run">
    Click the **Run** button to begin the simulation. The animated habitat map on the left will update decade by decade, while the biodiversity index graph on the right records the change in species richness over the simulation period.
  </Step>
  <Step title="View the Biodiversity Index and Habitat Map Over Time">
    Watch both outputs simultaneously. The habitat map shows land cover changes using color-coded categories (forest, grassland, wetland, urban, restored). The biodiversity index graph plots overall species richness and, for some scenarios, individual species' population trends.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compare Before and After Maps">
    When the simulation ends, click **Compare Maps** to view the starting landscape and the final landscape side by side. Use the overlay toggle to highlight which areas were lost, protected, or restored.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Comparing Scenarios

One of the most powerful features of the Habitat Change simulation is **Compare Mode**, which lets you run two scenarios back-to-back and view their results side by side.

To use Compare Mode, complete your first scenario run as normal, then click **Save & Compare** instead of simply saving. EcoLearn will hold your first run in memory and prompt you to configure a second run. You can change the scenario type entirely — for example, comparing Deforestation with Wetland Drainage — or keep the same scenario and vary a single parameter, such as doubling the Protected Area Size, to isolate its effect.

Once both runs are complete, the Compare View displays the two biodiversity index graphs overlaid on a shared time axis, with the two final habitat maps shown side by side. Use the annotation tool to mark up specific moments or areas on the maps before saving or sharing your comparison.

<Note>
  You can save any scenario result — including Compare Mode comparisons — using the **Save** button in the results panel. Give each saved run a descriptive name so you can distinguish them in your library. To share a run with your class or teacher, open it from **My Simulations** and click **Share** to copy a link or send it directly to your enrolled class. Sharing your results is a great way to spark discussion about different conservation strategies.
</Note>
